ComfyUI: advertising storyboards

ComfyUI Advertising Automation Consistency

ComfyUI: the pipeline architecture."> This post is a follow-up to What is ComfyUI, applied to a concrete case: an advertising mascot pair, Pannocchino and Spighetta, created for an Italian cake product. A brand built around two recurring characters cannot survive on a handful of illustrations — it needs a steady stream of new scenes, occasions, and formats, all showing the same two faces. Redrawing or re-photographing a mascot for every new story does not scale. What follows is a non-technical walkthrough of a ComfyUI pipeline built to generate many such stories automatically, keep the characters recognizable in every single shot, and catch and fix its own mistakes before a human ever sees them. The images below are concept illustrations used to visualize the idea — not output already produced by the pipeline described here.

  1. 01

    From a short brief to a full storyboard

    ComfyUI: the pipeline architecture."> Instead of writing one prompt per image and hoping it fits the previous one, the pipeline starts from a short, structured brief: who appears, what the occasion is, roughly how many shots the story needs, and what tone it should have — a birthday is not a harvest festival, and the brief says so explicitly rather than leaving it to be guessed shot by shot. That brief is expanded automatically into a full sequence of individual shot descriptions — framing, pose, setting, and the small continuity details that tie one frame to the next — the same way a human storyboard artist would break a script into panels, except the breakdown itself is generated rather than drawn by hand.

    This is the part that makes the system reusable rather than a one-off script. Changing the brief — a different occasion, a different setting, a different cast — is enough to start an entirely new story end to end; nothing about the characters themselves, or about the mechanism that keeps them consistent, needs to be touched. The brief is the only thing a person actually has to write.

  3. 02

    Staying recognizable, shot after shot

    A plain text-to-image generator redraws a character from scratch every time, which is why it drifts: a slightly different face, a different shade of yellow, a hairstyle that is close but not quite right. Across a handful of images the drift is barely noticeable; across dozens it becomes a visible, inconsistent brand. To avoid this, the pipeline keeps a separate visual reference for each named character — a kind of fingerprint of how they look, built once from a small set of approved images — and attaches it to every shot it generates, whether it is a wide establishing shot in a wheat field or a tight close-up like the one above.

    The reference does the same job in every scene, which is what keeps Pannocchino and Spighetta looking like themselves across dozens of images instead of only the first few. It also scales sensibly: the two main characters carry a strict identity-lock, while secondary figures — farm animals, background guests, anyone not central to the brand — can appear from a plain description alone, without needing a reference of their own. Precision is spent where the brand actually depends on it, not evenly across every pixel in frame.

  5. 03

    A self-checking step, not a human one

    Keeping the characters on-model is not guaranteed on the first attempt — it is checked. After each shot is generated, a second, independent pass looks at the result and compares it against the brief: are the right characters present, in the right pose, nothing off-brand in frame? That second pass is itself a generative model, asked to act as a reviewer rather than an illustrator — the same kind of judgment a human art director would apply, but running automatically on every single shot instead of a sample of them.

    When something does not match, that one shot is quietly regenerated and checked again, in a small loop that keeps running on its own until it passes — or is flagged for a human to look at, if it fails repeatedly. This is what makes it practical to introduce something as demanding as a new character in frame — like the scene above — without a human manually approving every output one by one. It is also the step that most clearly separates this approach from asking a general-purpose image tool for one picture at a time: there, the equivalent check is a person looking at the result and deciding by eye whether to accept it or try again. That works fine for one image. It does not stay fast or cheap once the brief calls for the twentieth, the fiftieth, or the two-hundredth.[3]

  7. 04

    One occasion, several scenes

    The brief does not have to describe a single, linear sequence of shots — it can branch. The two images above are not two different stories: they are the same occasion, the first birthday, told twice. One version stays close to the family, indoors, just the three of them around the cake. The other steps outside, widens the frame, and brings in the farmyard — a cow, a dog, a handful of chickens — for a livelier, more populated version of the same afternoon. Both are generated from the same underlying brief with one parameter changed, and both go through the identical pipeline: expanded into shots, generated, and checked against the brief before being accepted.

    This is where the identity-lock and the background-character handling described above actually earn their keep. The farmyard variant needs the animals treated as background figures — prompt-only, no reference, no identity-lock, exactly as in point 02 — while the three named characters still carry their strict reference in every shot, farmyard or living room. Branching a single occasion into two deliverable scenes costs nothing extra in setup: it is the same mechanism, run twice with a different setting, not a second production.

  9. 05

    Why this is fast, cheap, and built to grow

    Three things follow directly from working this way — and all three are best understood against the alternative most content creators and agencies actually use today: asking a general-purpose tool like Gemini for one image at a time, by hand, restarting from the same reference each time to keep some rough continuity. That approach is not wrong — for a single hero image it is often the simplest thing to do — but it does not get cheaper or faster as volume grows, because the part that does not scale is the person: writing each prompt, judging each result, deciding what to regenerate.

    • Many variations. Because a brief — not a fixed drawing — is what changes between stories, the same cast can appear in dozens of different occasions (a birthday, an afternoon in the farmyard, a harvest festival, a new product launch) without anything being redesigned from scratch. One-shot generation can reach the same scenes eventually, but each one is its own manual effort, re-prompted and re-judged from nothing.
    • Low cost that stays low. There is no reshoot and no new illustration commissioned for each variant; cost scales with a few minutes of computer time rather than hours of an illustrator's, photographer's, or prompt-engineer's time. The one-shot method also looks cheap at first — a single image is nearly free — but its real cost is the person re-prompting and eyeballing every output, and that cost is paid again, in full, for every single image. The pipeline's cost is paid mostly once, up front, in building the character reference and the checking step; after that, each additional story is marginal.
    • Built to be rebuilt. Every step of the mechanism — the brief, the character reference, the checking step — is a separate, explicit piece. A new character, a new kind of occasion, or a new check can be added later without starting over, unlike a finished video or a commissioned illustration, which has to be redone whole if something changes — and unlike a one-shot workflow, which has no pieces to begin with: it is a person and a tool, not a system that can be inspected, versioned, or handed to someone else to run.

    None of this makes one-shot generation obsolete — for a single campaign image it remains the fastest path from idea to picture, and it is the right choice when there is only ever going to be one image. The case for a pipeline only appears once the brand needs the same characters again, and again, and again: that is when the manual-supervision cost of the one-shot method stops being a rounding error and starts being the whole budget, while the pipeline's cost curve has already gone flat.
