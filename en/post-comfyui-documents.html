ComfyUI: curated online resources

ComfyUI Documentation Tutorial Video

A curated selection of online resources for ComfyUI: official repositories, workflow libraries, and video channels. Each entry is annotated — scope, technical level, and the most relevant use case are indicated — rather than offered as a bare list of links. The page is divided into two sections: web documentation and video tutorials.

  1. 01

    Web documentation (annotated)

    The official ComfyUI documentation is deliberately minimal. A more practical reference is the community repository at github.com/comfyanonymous/ComfyUI, where the README and Issues section together cover most setup and installation questions. For workflow examples, ComfyUI_examples collects tested pipelines for the most common use cases — text-to-image, image-to-image, inpainting, ControlNet, upscaling. External resources worth bookmarking: ComfyUI Manager, a sub-environment for discovering and installing new and/or custom nodes (not easy to use if you're not an expert: it helps to get an LLM's assistance, asking via chat how to solve a specific problem in order to fix or enrich a graph). The Civitai library for downloading models through checkpoints and, in certain cases, LoRA customisations trained on specific datasets. We also recommend the subreddit r/comfyui for troubleshooting (that is, advice on solving problems that other users have already encountered) — a pre-LLM style resource, but still consistently useful for broadening one's knowledge of the environment.

  2. 02

    Video tutorials

    The vast majority of YouTube tutorials presenting ComfyUI applications aim primarily to grow their own audience, favouring spectacle over explanation. Genuinely educational channels — oriented towards presenting pipelines with functions typical of the worlds of advertising, art direction and VFX — remain rare. The reason for this asymmetry is straightforward. The generative AI market today is not dedicated to creating storyboards or visual effects, but to light entertainment in one's free time or to completing school assignments (at home and in class).
    The YouTube landscape broadly reflects this imbalance. There is practically no middle ground between the purely decorative channel — enjoyable to watch but offering no real learning value — and the specialist channel, such as those listed below. Since AI is a phenomenon of dizzying expansion, doing new things with it requires commitment, experimentation, repeated attempts and staying up to date. On this site I have tried to reach a level that is sufficiently accessible while still maintaining an explanatory approach to how things are actually built within visual stories (see the posts ComfyUI Visual Stories — Part 1 and ComfyUI Visual Stories — Part 2). I would finally like to present a selection of top channels, to give a sense of the standard set by those who genuinely know how to work well in this field.

    • Summary. If the goal is to build a ComfyUI course for filmmakers, Mickmumpitz is closest to cinematic pre-production. Sebastian Kamph is the best for explaining nodes from scratch. TensorAlchemist is useful for video generation on modest GPUs. Latent Vision is a valuable archive, but stalled at 2023-2024.

    • Mickmumpitz The most useful for cinema, but he explains flows more than nodes.
      54 videos, roughly one a month.
      • ComfyUI: of 30 recent videos, 15 are explicit about it (3 from the description, 12 from the title). One is clearly something else (Blender + ChatGPT), and 14 are ambiguous but, in all likelihood, ComfyUI (inference).
      • Themes: character consistency, AI rendering from Blender, filmmaking pipelines.
      • Originality: high on the flows, which are designed by him (RunComfy credits him as the author of the Blender-ComfyUI renderer). The models are community models, and no original nodes of his own show up.
      • Distribution: a free base on his own site, extras (advanced flows, LoRA) on Patreon. Two of the most recent videos are NVIDIA-sponsored.
      • Teaching: chapters, an installation guide, a real transcript. In the masterclass he explains structure, GGUF, LoRA and setup, but not the individual nodes (about two thirds of the transcript was read).
      • Limit: the flows depend on models that age within months.
    • Sebastian Kamph The best teacher of nodes, but ComfyUI is a minor part of his teaching.
      182K subscribers, 255 videos.
      • ComfyUI: of the 30 most recent, only 8 (about 27%) are explicitly about it. 15 are reviews of commercial tools (Kling, Seedance, Genspark, InVideo and similar), and 6 are open models, possibly in ComfyUI (unverified).
      • Originality: he explains other people's tools, no original nodes of his own. The full course comes out first on Patreon and then on YouTube. Floyo is promoted with no visible sponsor disclosure.
      • Teaching: in chapter 9 he genuinely explains nodes, connector colors, latents and VAE, at a slow, beginner-friendly pace.
      • For cinema: little. No playlist on storyboards, consistent characters or continuity between shots.
    • Latent Vision An authoritative source, by now an archive.
      Matteo Spinelli, author of ComfyUI_IPAdapter_plus, InstantID and essentials. 38.6K subscribers, 34 videos.
      • ComfyUI: 28 of the 30 listed (from the titles).
      • Dates: the last upload is from March 12, 2026, “Why I ditched ComfyUI for Diffusers”. Everything else is 2023-2024. Since April 14, 2025 the main repository has been in maintenance mode only.
      • Originality: the highest of the five, because he explains nodes he wrote himself.
      • Limits: it was SD1.5/SDXL and AnimateDiff, nothing on current video models and nothing aimed at cinema.
      • Teaching: the chapters (IPAdapter v2: ten segments) suggest a tour of features for someone who already knows ComfyUI (inference). Transcripts not read, so clarity for non-developers is not verified.
    • Tensor Alchemist Effective on an 8 GB GPU, but skewed toward whatever is newest.
      A young channel (subscribed since December 19, 2025): 82 videos. Anonymous author, stated country Sri Lanka.
      • ComfyUI: of the 30 most recent, 14 yes, 10 no (cloud, ChatGPT, Claude, Higgsfield, Kling), 6 ambiguous.
      • Originality: assembles and optimizes community nodes and models for 8 GB of VRAM, with precise credits and no original nodes of his own.
      • Teaching: free workflows on Google Drive, licensing warnings, mistakes shown on screen, honesty about limits. The inpainting video explains why the parameters are set as they are; the character-sheet video is more of a timed trial run than a lesson on nodes. Fast pace: a non-technical filmmaker needs the text alongside it.
      • For cinema: useful material (character sheets, first/last-frame guides, continuity between clips), but no storyboard pipeline.
    • Simone Rizzo A channel written and run by an Italian :)
      Simone Rizzo, AI engineer, graduate in Computer Engineering with a specialization in Artificial Intelligence, CEO of Inferentia and adjunct professor at the University of Bologna.
      • ComfyUI: the channel does have ComfyUI content, but it is old and marginal compared to his recent activity. Installation from comfy.org, then the library of ready-made templates (Flux, Omnigen, Cosmos, Wan, paid API models, ControlNet with pose/depth/area composition, 3D models, audio). No workflow of his own.
      • Teaching: the fundamental concepts (CLIP/VAE/KSampler/denoising) are actually named and explained, if only in passing — it's not just “download and click”. HORROR!!!!!!! he does say ComfyUI is for “using Midjourney, Flux, SDXL, LoRA, ControlNet” — but Midjourney cannot be integrated into ComfyUI, it has no official API, it's a closed service via Discord with no connection whatsoever to the ComfyUI panel (a separate point: it's still very much used in disposable advertising, but conceptually it's a kind of development heading toward a dead end, it's not integrable, it simply doesn't reflect the basic principles of software integration of the last 30 years. It survives only because the worlds of developing and graphic creation are, for a little while longer, culturally distinct. Even though operationally they no longer are).
      • For cinema: marginal. No mention of storyboards, consistent characters or video; it's a tour of ready-made templates, not a lesson on hand-built nodes.
