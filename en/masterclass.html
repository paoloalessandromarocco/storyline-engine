Create HYPERREALISTIC Consistent AI Characters

ComfyUI Mickmumpitz LoRA AI Toolkit

This page breaks down Mickmumpitz's masterclass Create HYPERREALISTIC Consistent AI Characters (761,885 views as of 11/14/2026, published October 7, 2025), referenced in this site's sitography. The stack used in the video is open source and runs locally: let's walk through the key points for building the full ComfyUI pipeline that generates the character's final video.

  • Starting point — a single image. From a single input image (in any style), the “Consistent Character Creator” (CCC) workflow — essentially a subgraph of the main pipeline, built on top of Qwen Image Edit's nodes — generates character variations: different poses, expressions, outfit changes (virtual try-on), new scenes and settings.
  • Qwen Image Edit1 — the engine behind the CCC. It's an instruction-based image editing model: starting from a reference character, it varies pose, outfit, and setting through text instructions, modifying an existing image rather than generating one from scratch.
  • Building the dataset. The workflow's second section is the Dataset Creation group, which “will create the captions and the upscaled images of your character” — the video's own words (minute 5:13). Every generated image is saved in the folder dedicated to the character, together with its caption file. The final dataset is thus a list of image-text pairs, the standard format accepted by any LoRA trainer. On top of that there's an upscaling module: Qwen Image Edit generates at a limited native resolution (up to 1024×1024 pixels). For a dataset meant to train a “hyperrealistic” LoRA — where fine detail in skin, texture, and fabric matters — generating at a lower resolution and upscaling before saving is the practice recommended in ComfyUI itself, because it lets the LoRA train on sharper, more detailed images.
  • AI Toolkit (Ostris) — the engine that runs the LoRA fine-tuning. The dataset is passed to AI Toolkit to train a LoRA dedicated to the character, based on the images just prepared. The handoff from Qwen to Wan works because AI Toolkit trains the LoRA directly targeting Wan 2.2 as the destination model (a requirement that's anything but a given, since the output weights have to match the architecture of the video generator used in the next step).
  • Wan 2.2 — final generation of images and video. The trained LoRA is used with Wan 2.2 to generate the final images and, through a dedicated workflow, the video sequences. Wan 2.2's Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture shows up directly in ComfyUI's node graph: unlike a traditional monolithic model (where you load one checkpoint and run a single sampling pass), Wan 2.2 splits the model into two separate expert networks (one for high-noise, one for low-noise), requiring the operator to manage several intermediate modules and manually tune two parallel branches. The basic generation process therefore doesn't happen in a single step, but in two sequential denoising passes over the same latent space, preceded by two parallel loading branches (plus a possible third, corrective pass in case of identity drift):
    1. Structure and motion (High-Noise Expert): the first sampling pass handles the initial, high-noise steps, where the model lays down the overall composition, body trajectories, and motion dynamics, stopping halfway without finishing the image cleanup.
    2. Detail and likeness (Low-Noise Expert): the second sampling pass takes the still partially noisy latent produced by the first pass and completes the final steps, reconstructing fine textures, skin, and facial features.
    3. Identity stabilization (optional/corrective step): if the face deforms during motion (identity drift), an extra constraint stage is needed (multi-frame conditioning, or a second low-denoise video-to-video pass) to realign the likeness.
    In video generation specifically, character consistency in Wan 2.2 I2V does tend to degrade during motion — a documented identity drift and facial-deformation problem addressed with dedicated guides, not a minor detail2. Another compositional approach is to pair the ComfyUI graph with pre-animation done in Blender: this split (Blender Grey-Box + control maps + Wan 2.2) applies an optimal division of labor, taking away from the diffusion model the task it's weakest and most stochastic at (holding geometric and perspective consistency in 3D space) and leaving it the task it excels at (atmospheric lighting, materials, skin texture, and the organic dynamics of fabric and hair). The overall difficulty is medium-high, not because it requires traditional VFX-artist skills, but because of the alignment between the two environments and the weight tuning in ComfyUI: the approach is strongly recommended for shots with spatial staging, complex camera moves (dolly shots, orbits, dolly-zoom, passes behind foreground objects), or choreography between multiple characters, while it's unnecessary overhead for static close-ups or scenes built only on facial micro-expressions3.
  • RunPod — cloud execution. Used in two stages: (a) for LoRA training, and (b) to run the entire ComfyUI pipeline through to the final video generation. RunPod is a cloud platform that lets you rent GPU/CPU instances on demand, choosing among dozens of configurations by power and hourly rate. For running ComfyUI, RunPod offers official templates that pre-install the environment and the ComfyUI Manager plugin in several hardware variants, including a dedicated template (“WAN 2.2 Text to Video”) already configured for Wan 2.2. Once the pod is up, you reach the ComfyUI interface through the browser and run the whole graph there — CCC, dataset creation, and final generation with Wan 2.2 — exactly as you would locally, just on cloud hardware.

    A cost estimate, calculated from current RunPod prices cross-checked against public benchmarks:

    • LoRA training (a static character with 28 variants, as in the video): $1.50–17, calculated from official RunPod prices × 2–4 GPU hours extrapolated from a documented real-world case (28 images, 2,500 steps, ~2.2 hours on an RTX 5090).
    • CCC + Dataset Creation pipeline (3–4 runs): $2–12. The L40 (48 GB, $0.82/h) turns out to be the smarter pick — it costs less than the RTX 4090 and offers double the VRAM, keeping Qwen Image Edit from running out of the 4090's 24 GB, as shown in real-world tests.
    • Video generation (5-second clips, 3–4 runs): under $0.10 with a 4-step accelerator on a 24 GB GPU (71 seconds to produce a clip); about $0.40–0.50 without an accelerator, still on 24 GB (20 steps, 8.5 minutes per clip). The real cost jump happens when VRAM isn't enough and the system has to offload and reload data mid-run: in those cases even budget GPUs can take over an hour for a single clip, pushing the total cost to $3–4 for a batch of 3–4 clips — not because of the hourly rate, but because of the time lost. A larger-capacity GPU (e.g. 80 GB) avoids this slowdown and, despite the higher hourly cost, can end up cheaper overall.

    A rigorous estimate of the total cost is hard to pin down, because each phase is independent: adding up every minimum or every maximum wouldn't give a realistic range. A rough average lands around $10 to produce three 5-second clips. That's the cost of building and running a custom pipeline — with a LoRA dedicated to a character placed in a dynamic setting — and it isn't directly comparable, one to one, to the per-video price of an API service like Nano Banana or Seedream, which includes no training on the subject at all.

    Automatic correction. The pipeline described in the video has no automated output-evaluation step: every generation has to be checked by eye. A generate→evaluate→correct loop would matter even more here than in pipelines for static images only: video is more exposed to temporal defects (like the identity drift described in note 2), and an error caught only at the end of processing costs far more, in both time and GPU resources, than one caught on a single frame.

  2. 02

    The reference models, a year on

    Every model named in the masterclass has since been superseded. The picture as of November 14, 2026, sourced where possible:

    • Editing, local: Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 — free, Apache-2.0 license, with character-identity consistency improved over the version shown in the video.
    • Editing, local (alternative): FLUX.2 klein 4B — free, Apache-2.0 license, about 13 GB of VRAM. Its distinctive feature is combining both text-to-image and editing in a single checkpoint (instead of requiring two separate models). In the pipeline it's an alternative to Qwen-Image-Edit for generating the character's dataset: FLUX.2 only produces the starting images, while the LoRA is still trained on Wan 2.2. So no direct architectural compatibility is needed between FLUX.2 and Wan — the generated images just need to be good enough for the training that follows.
    • Editing, cloud, paid: Nano Banana 2.1 and Seedream 5.5 — at the top of the quality range among commercial models, both API-only.
    • Video, local: LTX-2.5 — open weights, free under $10M in annual revenue, 16 GB VRAM minimum. Wan 2.2 remains the last Wan release with open weights; later versions (2.5, 2.6, 3.0) are API-only.
    • Video, cloud, paid: Seedance 2.0, Kling 3.0, Veo 3.1 — among the most-cited commercial models for visual quality.

    The masterclass's basic architecture still holds up: ComfyUI, the CCC workflow, and one LoRA for the lead character. It's worth swapping in Qwen Image Edit 2511 and weighing LTX-2.5 alongside Wan 2.2 for the video stage, keeping in mind that models released only a few weeks ago don't yet have a consolidated independent verification.

  1. Qwen-Image-Edit is an image-editing model developed by Alibaba's Qwen team, released August 19, 2025, built on top of the 20-billion-parameter Qwen-Image backbone under an Apache 2.0 license (open source, commercial use included; Product Hunt). According to the technical documentation, Qwen-Image uses a three-module architecture (NVIDIA):
    • Qwen2.5-VL (7 billion parameters): a “frozen” (not retrained) multimodal language model, used to extract text-image features and for semantic alignment; it belongs to the well-known Qwen language-model family, in its vision-language variant, able to process images and text together.
    • VAE (encoder/decoder, 54M/73M parameters): a compact module tasked with preserving the fidelity of visual detail and pixels.
    • MMDiT (Multimodal Diffusion Transformer, 20 billion parameters): the actual generative engine — the diffusion transformer that synthesizes the final image.
    Working together, Qwen-Image-Edit sends the input image simultaneously to Qwen2.5-VL (for semantic control) and to the VAE's encoder (for visual consistency), combining text-driven semantic editing with fidelity to the original appearance. In practice: Qwen2.5-VL reads the image and the text instruction and decides what to change conceptually (for instance, “put a hat on the cat”); the VAE preserves the visual detail of the areas the edit doesn't touch; the MMDiT then synthesizes the result, balancing both information flows. ↩
  2. Wan 2.2 14B isn't a single-pass model like Qwen Image Edit or FLUX, but a Mixture-of-Experts made up of a high-noise expert (structure and motion) and a low-noise expert (detail); AI Toolkit trains both through its multi-stage mode, which means a single training run has to produce and manage two distinct sets of weights rather than one. At inference time in ComfyUI, there's no single “all-in-one” node — instead, a multi-module graph of at least 7–8 core nodes wired in series and in parallel (two Load Diffusion Model nodes, at least two LoraLoaderModelOnly nodes, the WanImageToVideo node, and two KSamplerAdvanced nodes cascaded with the intermediate latent passed between them). This architecture means the operator has to manually step in at four critical junctures in the graph: (1) duplicating the LoRA nodes across both branches and calibrating their strengths separately (generally keeping the weight low on the high-noise branch so as not to stiffen the motion, and raising it on the low-noise branch to lock in the likeness); (2) filtering LoRA compatibility between the branches (LoRAs for Wan 2.1 and the low-noise LoRAs for Wan 2.2 remain usable on the second branch, while high-noise-specific LoRAs must be excluded from the low-noise branch to avoid artifacts); (3) tuning the exact handoff step between the first and second sampler; (4) countering, in Image-to-Video (I2V) mode, the documented identity drift problem, since during large movements or head turns the face tends to progressively deform relative to the starting frame, forcing the operator to add intermediate anchoring modules (such as Start/End Frame constraints or multi-view references) or to fall back on corrective post-processing passes. ↩
  3. When it's worth pairing Blender with Wan 2.2, and when it isn't:
    Shot type Recommended pipeline Reason
    Moving camera (orbits, dolly shots, spatial continuity, focal-length changes) Blender + maps + Wan 2.2 Pure AI loses track of the environment's geometry; Blender guarantees correct parallax and continuity on the first attempt.
    Complex interaction (two subjects crossing paths, hands grabbing objects) Blender + maps + Wan 2.2 Avoids anatomical interpenetration and deformed limbs during motion.
    Emotional close-up / dialogue (static camera or slight push-in) Keyframes only + Wan 2.2 I2V A rough 3D animation of the face would look unnatural and would reduce the model's expressiveness.
    Pure atmosphere / landscapes (wind, smoke, water, ambient light) Keyframes only + Wan 2.2 I2V The model's native stochastic physics is far richer and faster to iterate on than going through 3D.
