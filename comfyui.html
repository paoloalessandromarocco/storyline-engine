ComfyUI

ComfyUI is a free, open-source tool for building generative-AI workflows visually, by connecting small building blocks — nodes — into a pipeline, rather than typing into one locked product's chat box. A closed system (Gemini, ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot and the like) hands you one interface and one way of working, set entirely by that company: the models, the features, the pricing and the roadmap all change on its schedule, not yours. An open pipeline hands you the pieces instead — models, samplers, control tools — and lets you wire them together however a specific job needs, mixing open and commercial components freely and swapping any one of them later without rebuilding the rest. For the technical side — nodes, graph, what each component does — see What is ComfyUI; what follows here is why that openness matters.

  1. 01

    Not exposed to the strategy of a handful of big companies

    OpenAI and Anthropic have both confidentially filed for a U.S. IPO in 2026 — Anthropic targeting a listing after the November midterms at a reported valuation above $2 trillion, OpenAI shortly after at a reported target near $1 trillion — and both are losing money at a scale that is no longer speculative: Anthropic's prospectus shows a $42 billion net loss on $4.6 billion of 2025 revenue, OpenAI's a loss in the $38–60 billion range on $13 billion of revenue. Neither company hides that today's flagship subscription tiers are sold below cost on their heaviest users: Sam Altman has said outright that OpenAI is "currently losing money on openai pro subscriptions," and OpenAI's own head of ChatGPT has called a flat, unlimited plan something the company "stumbled into" rather than designed to last.

    History suggests what happens next. Lyft and Uber both tightened subsidies and adjusted pricing within months of their 2019 IPOs; Peloton's clearest move, in 2022, was to cut hardware prices while raising its subscription fee for the first time in eight years — reallocating margin onto the recurring layer rather than announcing one blanket hike. The same pattern is already visible in AI tools adjacent to this one, pre-IPO: Cursor, GitHub Copilot and Anthropic's own Claude Code all moved from flat-rate or "unlimited" access to usage caps or metered billing during 2025, and independent analysts estimate that genuine break-even on today's cheapest tiers would require something like 5 to 10 times current prices. A small studio or freelancer paying $10–50 a month for a closed AI product is, in effect, paying a price these companies have already signalled they consider temporary.

    A node-based open pipeline doesn't remove that risk, but it isn't structurally exposed to it the same way: the models, the compute and the pricing are choices made shot by shot, not a single subscription tier owned by one company's board. A commercial API can still be one node in the graph — but it's one node among several, swappable the day its price or its terms change, rather than the whole workflow.

  2. 02

    General-purpose by design, at a lower scaling cost

    A closed product is one interface built around whatever that company decided to ship: a chat box, a fixed set of features, a handful of presets. A ComfyUI graph is built from interchangeable nodes — model loader, conditioning, sampler, ControlNet, IP-Adapter and so on — so the same underlying pipeline can be rewired for a completely different job by swapping a handful of nodes, rather than by waiting for a vendor to ship a new feature, or by paying for a different product altogether. That versatility carries a cost advantage at scale: a closed product's price generally follows its own tier structure, which is the company's commercial decision as much as a reflection of actual compute used. In an open pipeline, scaling up a batch of generations mostly means more of your own compute time, at close to its real marginal cost — not a renegotiated contract or a jump to the next pricing tier.

  3. 03

    Where the real saving shows up: volume, not the first shot

    This last point isn't specific to ComfyUI — it holds for AI-assisted production generally. For a short storyboard of around 10 shots (see the examples in Visual Stories), an illustrator working by hand with an integrated toolset is still faster than setting up and running an AI pipeline for it — the fixed cost of configuring characters, style and consistency checks outweighs what it saves on so few shots. That stops being true once the batch grows to 50 or 100 shots. By then the pipeline's setup cost has already been paid once, and each additional shot costs close to nothing extra to generate; hand-made production has no equivalent discount — the hundredth frame still costs an illustrator roughly as much time as the first one. The AI pipeline's advantage isn't that it's cheaper per shot from the start; it's that its marginal cost keeps falling while manual production stays essentially linear.
