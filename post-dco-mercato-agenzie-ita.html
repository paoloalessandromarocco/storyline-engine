The Agency Channel in Italy

DCO Go-to-Market Agencies Italy Market Analysis ADV

This post gives an overview of: who operates in the Italian DCO (Dynamic Creative Optimization) market, who buys from the providers/agencies offering the service, where a small producer can realistically enter, and what a flexible service shared by several agencies would look like. In short, the realistic way in: not a new product that could compete with the platforms. The international picture — what traditional DCO can and can't do, what the large platforms' AI already covers, and the realistic route into the gap — is written up in DCO and the Agency Market.

  1. 01

    Who operates in the Italian DCO market

    Companies offering Dynamic Creative Optimization in Italy split cleanly into three tiers, and the split tracks what each one actually sells. The first tier is platform infrastructure: Criteo, through its Milan branch, runs DCO+, an AI-driven engine that assembles banners and video ads in real time from product feeds and browsing-behaviour signals — a commerce-media retargeting business, not a creative-production one. Connected-Stories, a native Italian-founded player with offices in Rome, Milan and New York, sells a Creative Management Platform built on predictive and generative AI for hyper-personalised dynamic video and display across CTV and the open web. Amazon Ads and Google's DV360/Studio sit in the same tier at enterprise scale, with DCO built into the buying platform itself rather than sold as a separate service.

    The second tier is independent AdTech and resellers (Eikontech, ShowHeroes Group, Aryel): technical integrators that make the first tier's platforms work inside a single campaign.

    The third tier is where creative decisions are made and executed: the Big Four media-agency hubs — WPP (through GroupM, EssenceMediacom, Wavemaker), Publicis, Omnicom and Dentsu Italy — run the DCO campaigns of enterprise clients through platforms like Flashtalking, Celtra or Google Studio, and, below them, a layer of specialised digital agencies (the names that come up are Youthquake, Hej! and Fragos Media) runs the same kind of performance-driven DCO integration for mid-market clients.

  2. 02

    Who actually buys it

    DCO in Italy gets adopted wherever a brand has a large product catalogue, prices that change often, or a high volume of individual customer touchpoints — the three conditions under which swapping creative elements automatically actually pays for itself. E-commerce and large retail is the clearest case (Unieuro, MediaWorld, Zalando, Amazon Italy, Yoox): catalogues too large to hand-design around. Automotive (Stellantis Group, BMW Italia, Volkswagen Group Italia) follows the same logic with location, weather and dealer offers instead of a live feed; fashion and luxury (Armani, Moncler, Calzedonia Group) use it for localised overlays rather than full personalisation, since the category guards its visual identity more tightly. Telecoms and energy, travel and hospitality, finance and insurance (TIM, Enel, ITA Airways, Generali, among others) use it for dynamic pricing, live offers and weather-triggered creative. What they all share is volume: enough touchpoints that no human workflow could produce a variant for each one.

  3. 03

    The open door: mid-market specialised agencies

    Of the three tiers mapped above, the first two are not realistic doors to walk through at a craft scale. Platform infrastructure — Criteo's DCO+, Google's DV360/Studio, native players like Connected-Stories — owns the data feeds, the targeting signals and the real-time serving layer; that's infrastructure, not a creative challenge. The Big Four agency hubs run enterprise accounts (the Stellantis, Armani, TIM-scale logos from the client map above) through years of sunk-cost integration with Flashtalking or Celtra; pitching a replacement into that relationship misreads what it actually is: a long-term platform and media-buying commitment.

    The third tier is where a subcontracted creative-generation vendor is a plausible fit: the specialised digital agencies named in the market map above — Youthquake, Hej!, Fragos Media — serving mid-market clients without a platform relationship as large as the Big Four's. They're named here as an illustration of the tier's shape, not as specific prospects — the point is structural: these shops run project-based engagements, commission specialists for production work they don't staff in-house, and serve brands closer to the food & beverage, wine and regional FMCG profile this pipeline's own case studies (Pannocchino e Spighetta, the vineyard pair) already match. That combination — smaller sales cycle, project-shaped engagement, a client base with mascot- or character-driven creative needs — is what makes this tier reachable where the first two aren't.

  4. 04

    The realistic route: calibration and audit, sold with onboarding

    On current evidence, the realistic way in is not a product but a service: calibration, audit and spot-check of AI creative checks, sold by the project to the mid-market agencies and regional brands described in chapter 03. Integrators and vendors are a later, harder step. The reason is a gap the vendors leave open. Platforms publish accuracy claims without a validation protocol behind them: Rocketium advertises 90%+ brand-compliance accuracy without disclosing how it was tested, and the Volkswagen pipeline built with AWS uses LLM judges for brand fit while its published account reports no agreement figure between judge and human experts1. Either way, per-brand calibration is a technical step the flexible engine needs anyway to be configured for that specific brand: it becomes the first step of onboarding itself, not a separate consulting engagement, because it's how the agency sees, from day one, how far it can trust the automatic check on its own creative.

    What would be proposed comes in three parts. A calibration study per brand: a person labels a set of shots as on/off-brand (30–50 to start, around 400 for a result worth highlighting), and the study then reports how often the judge agrees with the human result (Cohen's kappa) and how many bad shots it lets through2. Raw agreement alone can mislead when brand-fit calls are ambiguous: a 2025 study shows that standard validation can pick judges of up to 34% lower quality than the supervised one3. A blind audit of a judge already in use: a reviewer who has not seen its verdicts re-checks a sample of the shots it approved; the share they reject is the escape rate, defined in DCO Engine: technical description, and that same sample is also used to measure the time spent approving each shot and the residual-defect rate — the two measures that, together with API cost, make up the total cost per approved variant.

A Flexible Service for Small Agencies

The model that holds up is a flexible service. The same engine can serve five to ten small agencies at once, because everything that differs between them isn't in the development code: each brand arrives as a configuration file, a reference pair and a calibration set (see DCO Engine: technical description), while the loop, the manifest and the export are shared. Five to ten agencies at two or three campaigns each means ten to thirty campaigns a month through one engine. The fixed cost is low (see the detailed cost page); what grows with each new agency is the cost of onboarding its brands and, to a much smaller extent, the API cost. An approach like this also fits what the engine is for: brand-consistent, checked creative for agencies that have neither a big-brand production budget nor a reviewer for every variant.

For that to hold, four things have to be verified:

  • Onboarding a brand stays cheap. The configuration file is the service's lever; if every new brand needs bespoke customisation, the model collapses back into the dedicated service.
  • Calibration is done per brand. It cannot be left out or shared, so onboarding carries a real human-time cost that doesn't show up in any API cost.
  • Tenants are isolated. One agency's reference pair, configuration and outputs are never visible from another: shared engine, separate brands.
  • Human review carries real value, not a free step. The engine has three points in the flow where supervision is still needed (it isn't all generated-and-approved automatically by the judge — there are three checkpoints where a human has to look and decide, described in DCO Engine: technical description). Those three checkpoints don't disappear once the engine is shared across agencies: every campaign that passes through the system carries the same number of steps to check by hand, and with many small tenants that queue has to be sized and priced accordingly.

What the cost model leaves open is the platform's fixed cost and the revenue per campaign. Two of the four conditions are also still open on the technical side: tenant isolation and the sizing of the human-review queue. On the first point: one agency's reference pair, configuration and outputs must never be visible to another agency, even while sharing the same engine. Today the system probably organises data by brand (each brand has its own configuration and reference pair), but there is no layer above the brand yet — a "tenant key" — that would group brands by agency and guarantee that agency A can never see or touch anything of agency B's.

To close: an automatic, iterative storyboard pipeline for campaign narratives is technically achievable — several aspects of it are still being worked out. A privacy and security layer, a precision and reliability layer, a layer for operating at scale. Having a working base in this corner of automated creativity already means keeping a close eye on how fast AI is moving in this space. In short, the list of things still to be addressed shouldn't be read as a threat, but as an opportunity to make the best use of the newest generative AI models and versions for images and video.
